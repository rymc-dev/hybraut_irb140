\chapter*{Abstract}

This report presents the integration and evaluation of the Robot Operating System (ROS) with an
ABB IRB140 industrial manipulator controlled by an IRC5 controller. A reproducible ROS-Industrial
platform was established using ROS Melodic, \texttt{abb\_driver}, a direct Ethernet connection,
controller-side motion and state servers implemented in ABB's RAPID programming language, and an
IRB140 robot description. The resulting system supports live joint-state feedback, action-based
joint-trajectory commands, MoveIt motion planning, RViz and Foxglove visualization, colour and
depth data from an Intel RealSense D405 depth camera, and rosbag-based experiment recording.

Alongside the real system, we developed a digital twin of the robotic cell -- with millimetre-accurate
world object meshes added based on exact measurements, and an IRB140 URDF for loading into Gazebo
with a ROS2 controller -- so that the simulated arm mimics the behaviour of the real IRB140. We also
designed and built a custom pneumatic end effector for the IRB140, enabling us to test simple
industrial pick-and-place tasks using the ROS integration, and performed a performance analysis of
the system to evaluate how it responds to commands.

The Robot Operating System (ROS) is a widely adopted open-source middleware framework for
robotic software development. Its modular topic, service and action interfaces allow developers
to combine hardware drivers with reusable packages for perception, planning, control and
visualization. There are a few reasons ROS was chosen for this project:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ecosystem and integration:} the MoveIt motion planning library provides robust
  trajectory planning and collision avoidance out of the box, extensive sensor integration makes
  it easy to bring the D405 camera into the system, and the standardized URDF representation makes
  it straightforward to convert CAD models to a robot description.
  \item \textbf{Open source and research-oriented:} ROS is community supported, well documented
  for industrial arms, and supports reproducibility and transparency for academic work; Gazebo
  simulation also aligns closely with the real, URDF-based system.
  \item \textbf{Middleware benefits:} ROS decouples the development pipeline for control, enabling
  distributed processing across perception, planning and control with standardized message
  formats for inter-component communication. ABB provides official ROS drivers proven in research
  and industrial settings, and the skills involved transfer to other collaborative manipulators
  (UR, KUKA).
\end{itemize}

We use ROS1 rather than ROS2 because the IRB140 platform's controller does not have the EGM
component required by the ROS2 \texttt{abb\_robot\_driver}, so we instead use the older
\texttt{abb\_driver}, which does not have this requirement. The alternatives to ROS were also
considered and ruled out:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{ABB RAPID (native programming):} tightly coupled to the controller, and limits
  sensor fusion and modularity for vision-based sorting tasks.
  \item \textbf{ABB FlexPendant GUI:} too limited for automated research applications.
  \item \textbf{Drake (Toyota Research Institute):} excellent for simulation/optimization, but
  overkill here and less aligned with industrial arm drivers.
  \item \textbf{Webots/CoppeliaSim:} simulation-first, not ideal for controlling real hardware.
  \item \textbf{PyBullet:} a physics engine rather than middleware, and lacks a real-time control
  abstraction.
\end{itemize}

Other robotic platforms were also considered:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Universal Robots (UR+) or Techman:} integrated ecosystems, but less open -- you
  end up locked into their framework.
  \item \textbf{KUKA iiwa / KRL:} similar trade-offs to ABB RAPID.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Theory Analysis and Design}

The ABB IRB140 is a 6-axis industrial robot with a payload of 6\,kg, making it a versatile robot
that is easy to integrate into many industrial applications: it has an open structure designed for
flexible use, and communication options that enable communication with many different platforms,
including potentially ROS.

Use cases for this robot include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Assembly
  \item Material handling and pick-and-place
  \item Machine tending
  \item Arc welding
  \item Surface finishing \& machining
  \item Spraying \& dispensing
\end{itemize}

Some notable companies that use the IRB140 include the BMW Group, who use the IRB140 Foundry Plus
for critical auxiliary foundry tasks: equipped with pneumatic gravers, it automatically engraves
date, time and quality-tracking status onto hot, freshly cast aluminum. AstraZeneca also use it for
consumer medical goods -- the high-speed version (IRB140T), paired with vision systems, manages the
rapid picking, sorting and packaging of pharmaceuticals (e.g.\ Rhinocort and Nexium) and boxes.

There are also specialized variants of this robot platform with similar interfaces, such as the
Foundry Plus 2/TF, which has IP67-rated sealed electronics and a corrosion-resistant coating for
harsh foundry environments and die casting.

Ultimately, the global industrial landscape is experiencing a structural paradigm shift driven by demographic collapses,
the massive physical expansion of AI infrastructure, and the emergence of Physical AI. With the OECD projecting a net loss
of 15 million workers by 2030 and next-generation data centers driving an automation market projected to exceed \$61.9 billion,
robotics has transitioned from a discretionary efficiency tool to a macroeconomic necessity. This workforce deficit has established
a 94\% statistical correlation between labor shortages and subsequent robotic integration.
It has also accelerated the adoption of specialized, high-density automation systems capable of operating in extreme or sterile environments.
As factories increasingly rely on automation to absorb these global labor and infrastructure shocks, understanding the precise kinematic and
mechanical capabilities of small-footprint, high-reliability systems becomes paramount. Consequently, the following sections provide a rigorous technical
analysis of the ABB IRB 140, detailing its six degrees of freedom, structural kinematics, workspace envelopes, and specific mechanical configurations required to meet these evolving industrial demands.

\subsection*{Physical Structure Analysis of the IRB140}

The IRB140 has 6 DOF, controlled by 6 axes, each assigned a position mnemonic A--F for Axis 1--6.

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{./figures/theory/irb140_axis.png}
  \caption{fig: ABB IRB140 product manual axis diagram~\cite{irb140_product_manual}}
  \label{fig:abb-irb140-axis}
\end{figure}

Each of these axes can be referred to as a joint; for the remainder of this document I will refer
to them as Joints, to follow ROS conventions, so Axis 1--6 will be known as J1--J6. Each of these
joints is of Revolute type. J1 is the base rotation, rotating the arm around the vertical axis; J2
is the shoulder/arm movement joint; J3 is the elbow/upper-arm movement joint; J4 is the wrist
joint; J5 is wrist bending; and J6 is the wrist/end-effector rotation joint. ABB groups J1--J3 as
arm motion and J4--J6 as wrist motion.

In kinematic terms, our IRB140 is:
\[
\text{R--R--R--R--R--R}
\]
with joint variables:
\[
\mathbf{q} = [q_1, q_2, q_3, q_4, q_5, q_6]^{T}
\]

On the IRC5 controller, joint positions are reported as quaternion-based tool orientations plus a
translational position, while on ROS2 we work directly with joint angles in radians.

As for the links -- the physical interconnects between the joints -- we also have 6, denoted
Link~0 through Link~5:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Link 0:} the base, between Ground and J1 -- the stationary mounting structure.
  \item \textbf{Link 1:} the lower arm/arm housing, J1 $\rightarrow$ J2/J3 region; rotates around
  J1 and carries the shoulder mechanism.
  \item \textbf{Link 2:} the upper arm, J2/J3 $\rightarrow$ J4; the main long arm section, moving
  with the shoulder/elbow.
  \item \textbf{Link 3:} the wrist housing/forearm, J4 $\rightarrow$ J5/J6; rotates with Axis 4 and
  carries the wrist axes.
  \item \textbf{Link 4:} the wrist section, J5 $\rightarrow$ J6; provides wrist orientation.
  \item \textbf{Link 5:} the tool flange/turn disc, J6 $\rightarrow$ tool; the final rotating
  interface to the end effector.
\end{itemize}

One thing to note is that the IRB140 is not simply six rigid links connected by six visible axes,
but we can theoretically consider it this way, as this is also the convention used by ROS.

The robot's dimensions are specified in Fig.~\ref{fig:abb-irb140-dimensions}, with an overall
height of 810\,mm, a length of 595\,mm at the default joint orientation, and a width of 402\,mm.
These dimensions and axes give our robot a reach of 0.8\,m.

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{./figures/theory/irb140_dimensions.png}
  \caption{ABB IRB140 product manual dimensions diagram~\cite{irb140_product_manual}}
  \label{fig:abb-irb140-dimensions}
\end{figure}

As for mounting tools, the IRB140 has a tool flange adapter for universal mounting, with four
mounting holes for 4x M5, 7.5\,mm depth, mounting holes (Fig.~\ref{fig:abb-irb140-tool-flange-drawing}).

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{./figures/theory/irb140_tool_flange_mounting_holes.png}
  \caption{ABB IRB140 product manual tool flange diagram~\cite{irb140_product_manual}}
  \label{fig:abb-irb140-tool-flange-drawing}
\end{figure}

Integrating the IRB140 is quite simple: the robot has several connection point locations, and the
main connection points we discuss are as follows. On the back of the robot we have several
connections:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{R} = robot axis
  \item \textbf{MP} = Motor Power (servo motor electrical connections)
  \item \textbf{FB} = Feedback (encoder feedback signals)
  \item \textbf{H} = Hold/Brake (axis holding brake)
  \item \textbf{CS} = Control Signal
  \item \textbf{SMB} = Status/Motor Brake
  \item \textbf{AIR} = Pneumatic air connections
  \item \textbf{G} = Ground
  \item \textbf{S1} = Sensor signal
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Design}

A two-part design: a tool flange adapter neck, which adds spacing between the tool flange and the
base plate mounting the end-effector components. Since we are connecting pneumatic gripper
fingers, we need some space between the end of the robotic arm and an air hose in order to
connect it properly.

The easiest way to do this was to create a custom pneumatic gripper design. We already had
silicone gripper moulds in the lab, so by reusing the dimensions from the CAD drawings of some
other, previously designed grippers, we were able to design a pneumatic gripper to fulfil our
needs.

\subsection{Pneumatics Design}
\label{sec:pneumatics-design}

At the heart of our pneumatics design is an SMC SY5000 Series 5-port pneumatic solenoid valve, a directional
control valve with the following specification:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Coil:} low-wattage
  \item \textbf{Fittings:} one-touch fittings on ports A and B, G-thread standard
  \item \textbf{Fluid:} air
  \item \textbf{Operating pressure:} 0.15--0.7\,MPa
  \item \textbf{Operating temperature:} $-10$ to $+50\,^{\circ}\text{C}$
  \item \textbf{Body material:} die-cast aluminum and aluminum alloy
  \item \textbf{Seal material:} Hydrogenated Nitrile Rubber (H-NBR)
\end{itemize}

The finite state machine for the solenoid valve is shown below.

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  % TODO: replace with the actual FSM diagram (./figures/theory/smc_sy5000_series5_port_fsm)
  \placeholderfigure{Diagram pending: SMC SY5000 solenoid valve FSM}
  \caption{Finite state machine of the pneumatic control of the solenoid valve}
  \label{fig:smc-sy5000-series5-port-fsm}
\end{figure}

The FSM in Fig.~\ref{fig:smc-sy5000-series5-port-fsm} shows two states of interest:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{De-energized (default):} no voltage is applied to either side of the solenoid.
  \item \textbf{Bottom side energized:} pneumatic port A on the right side is linked to port A on the left
  side, and port B on the right side is linked to port B on the left side.
\end{itemize}

This routes air from the input port through to the output ports. For our purposes we only need to wire up
this one side of the solenoid, since a second air regulator sits downstream on the valve's output. When air
pressure is removed, this stage drains and the grippers deactivate; when pressure is applied, the grippers
activate. The valve's output is then split via a T-fitting into the inputs of both silicone gripper fingers.

The pneumatic wiring is laid out as follows:

\begin{center}
air source $\rightarrow$ regulator 1 (0.2\,MPa) $\rightarrow$ port B input of solenoid valve
$\rightarrow$ port B output of solenoid valve $\rightarrow$ regulator 2 (0.3\,MPa) $\rightarrow$
T-splitter $\rightarrow$ left and right pneumatic gripper fingers
\end{center}

\subsubsection{Electrical / IO Integration}

For electronic control from the IRC5 controller, we initially wanted to use the R2.CS port on the
IRB140 so that only a single circular connector and a simple wiring loom would be needed. After
investigating the available circuit diagrams and documentation, and opening the robot up to trace
the wiring directly, we found (using a multimeter) that some pins on R2.CS do carry a 24\,V
signal, but there is no way to drive them digitally by default from the IRC5 Compact pendant --
R2.CS is not a DeviceNet component out of the box, and enabling it would require additional
hardware/firmware changes to the IRC5.

Instead, gripper control is wired through XS14 and XS12: XS14 pin~1 is the digital output that
drives the grippers open/closed (24\,V for open, 0\,V for closed), XS14 pin~2 drives the grippers
open, and XS12 pin~9 provides the neutral return.

\subsection{Issues and Future Improvements}

This is a prototype version of the end effector design, and we had some issues when actually
assembling it. These issues were twofold: first, the reference sketches I used for the finger
mounts did not give the correct distance between the mounting holes on the sides of the finger
mounts, so in future the mounting arms need to be spaced approximately 2\,mm further apart to
solve this -- we drilled through the mounting hole on the finger mount by a tiny margin to give
enough leeway to assemble it. Secondly, we did not have the M4$\times$35\,mm screws specified by
the CAD assembly, so the tool flange end effector adapter neck should in future be made shorter to
account for a 30\,mm screw length instead; to solve this problem, we ground the 50\,mm M3 screws
down by 20\,mm so we could properly mount the end effector.

One final problem was that the silicone material of the fingers did not have a high pressure
tolerance, so we had to be extremely careful with the pressure regulators connected to the
solenoid valves to ensure the fingers did not burst. This was fine, but in future it may be
useful to use a stronger material to remove this concern, as bursting a finger during testing
would have been a costly setback.

\subsection{ROS2 control}

Control of the end effector is difficult. Simulating the robot in Gazebo requires two
controllers: one for the robotic arm, and another for the pneumatic gripper. The pneumatic
gripper model in simulation is a combination of several different joints per finger, similar to
a human finger, since we cannot simulate the silicone fingers' actual air-driven closing motion
directly -- the joint chain is used as an approximation instead.
